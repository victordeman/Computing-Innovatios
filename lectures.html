Lectures

Week 1

Introduction to Computing Innovation

This week introduces the fundamental difference between invention and innovation, the major types of innovation (incremental, radical, disruptive, and architectural), and why computing innovations have such enormous economic and societal impact. We will also provide a clear overview of the entire course and the key emerging technologies we will explore.

Reading: Drucker, Innovation and Entrepreneurship (Ch. 1–2).

Week 2

Innovation Frameworks and Processes

We examine practical frameworks for systematic innovation, including Design Thinking, Lean Startup principles, and Drucker’s seven sources of innovative opportunity. Students will learn how innovation can be managed as a disciplined process rather than relying on chance or genius alone.

Reading: Drucker, Innovation and Entrepreneurship (Ch. 3–10).

Week 2 Resources Week2.pdf (Coming soon)
Week 3

History of Major Computing Innovations

This session tells the human story of computing from the earliest mechanical ideas to today’s digital world — covering early computers, the transistor and microchip revolutions, the rise of personal computers, the internet, smartphones, and cloud computing. We highlight key innovators and the collaboration patterns that created modern technology.

Reading: Isaacson, The Innovators (selected chapters).

Week 3 Resources Week3.pdf (Coming soon)
Week 4

Case Study: Personal Computing

We dive into Clayton Christensen’s groundbreaking model of sustaining versus disruptive innovation and the “Innovator’s Dilemma.” Using classic computing cases (mainframes → PCs → mobile; on-premise servers → cloud), we explore why successful companies often fail when disruptive technologies appear.

Reading: Christensen, The Innovator’s Dilemma (core chapters).

Week 5

Artificial Intelligence & Machine Learning Innovations

This lecture explores current advances in AI and machine learning, including deep learning, generative AI, and intelligent agents. We discuss real-world applications, technical limitations, and the innovation pipeline from research labs to everyday products.

Supplementary: Recent articles from MIT Technology Review and NeurIPS summaries.

Week 6

Cloud, Edge, and Distributed Computing

We examine how cloud computing, edge computing, and distributed systems have transformed scalability and business models. Students will understand the shift from traditional infrastructure to modern serverless and edge-AI architectures and their real-world impacts.

Week 7

Blockchain, Decentralization, and Web3

This week introduces blockchain technology, smart contracts, cryptocurrencies, and decentralized autonomous organizations (DAOs). We go beyond finance to explore applications in supply chain, digital identity, and new forms of trust and ownership.

Week 8

Quantum Computing

Students receive a beginner-friendly introduction to quantum computing principles, current hardware state (qubits and quantum supremacy claims), potential breakthrough applications, and the significant technical and timeline challenges that remain.

Week 9

Internet of Things (IoT), Sensors, and Ubiquitous Computing

We explore how connected sensors and devices are creating smart homes, smart cities, and Industry 4.0. The lecture covers architectures, real-world deployments, and the critical data, security, and privacy challenges that accompany widespread IoT adoption.

Week 10

AR/VR/XR, Human-Computer Interaction, and Immersive Tech

This session covers augmented, virtual, and mixed reality (AR/VR/XR), spatial computing, and the evolution of human-computer interfaces. We examine how immersive technologies are reshaping experiences in education, entertainment, design, and collaboration.

Week 11

Cybersecurity, Privacy, and Trust Innovations

We discuss emerging threats and modern defenses, including zero-trust architectures, AI-driven security, and privacy-preserving techniques such as federated learning. The focus is on building trustworthy systems in an increasingly connected world.

Week 12

Ethics, Society, Policy, and Regulation

This week addresses the broader societal implications of computing innovation: algorithmic bias, job displacement, digital divide, surveillance, and professional ethics. We analyze real-world case studies and the role of policy and regulation in shaping responsible innovation.

Week 13

Entrepreneurship, Commercialization, and IP in Computing

Students learn how computing innovations move from idea to market. Topics include startup ecosystems, venture capital, intellectual property (patents vs. open source), go-to-market strategies, and the commercialization process.

We conclude with technology roadmapping, scenario planning, and forecasting methods for future computing innovations. The majority of the session is dedicated to student group project presentations, peer feedback, and final course reflections.
